The **High Boy** is an interesting device for people exploring electronics and hardware security. I started experimenting with a Raspberry Pi Zero W, and what matters to me is the knowledge behind the tools. Hacking is not about launching pre-made programs, pressing buttons, or following someone else's instructions. It involves understanding how hardware, software, networks and communication protocols work; examining their behavior, finding weaknesses, modifying systems and developing your own solutions. Building and configuring a server, for example, is a much more meaningful learning experience than simply running a tool without knowing what it does. At around R$700 for an ESP32-based device and a few accessories, I find the price steep. Still, I may buy one through AliExpress when it becomes available, mainly out of technical curiosity.

What interests me most are the possibilities for experimentation. During my Electrical Engineering studies, I was introduced to radio frequency concepts and the principles behind signal transmission and reception. I would like to explore these subjects further, including how to design and build radio communication systems. In Computer Science, I have also studied computing fundamentals, standardization and information security concepts. The High Boy can be a useful starting point for investigating how devices communicate, how protocols are implemented, and how systems can be tested responsibly.

I would recommend the High Boy to curious beginners, as long as they see it as an educational tool rather than a shortcut to becoming a hacker. A device can make experiments easier, but it cannot replace the ability to understand what is happening underneath. Using infrared to control a television or running an automated script may be entertaining, but those actions alone do not demonstrate hacking knowledge. The real value comes from analyzing, learning, building and experimenting with the technology itself.
